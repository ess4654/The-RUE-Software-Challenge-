\section{Presidential UFOs}\label{sec:presidentialufos}

When I first began research into the subject of UFOs there was one thing above all I could not
seem to shake from my desire to understand. Why? Why was there such contestable laughter and
egregious indignation of the public's opinion of the subject? It seemed to me as though aliens
and UFOs had been cast into the same bin of unspeakable taboos along with sex, suicide, mental
health and all the rest of the awkward and rarely spoken facts of human existence. The answer,
or most of it, is in this chapter --- because the ridicule was manufactured, on purpose, by
people whose names and memoranda we now have.

Before the men in black, before the black budget, before the missing files, start with a plain fact that most of the literature on both sides manages to bury: \hi{a President of the United
States filed a UFO report.} Not an aide, not an anonymous colonel, not a witness reached
through a researcher's intermediary. Jimmy Carter, in his own handwriting, on a form, with his
name and address on it, describing a light in the sky he could not identify --- and he did it
before he was president, which is the only reason we can read it.

That is the frame for this entire chapter, and it cuts in two directions at once. It means the
government is not a monolith of liars sitting on a secret: the highest office in that
government has itself been on the witness side of the desk. And it means the witness problem
does not stop at the top. \hi{A president can see something honestly, report it honestly, and
still be wrong about what it was} --- and Carter may or may not have been, which is not a
call this book gets to make, because his own testimony closes the question. Hold both of those
thoughts, because everything else in this chapter is a variation on them.

Every American president since Truman has been asked about UFOs, and the answers form a
surprisingly useful record --- not of what is in the vaults, but of what the office knows and
how it behaves when it does not know something.

\textbf{Truman}, 1950, in a press conference: ``I can assure you that flying saucers, given
that they exist, are not constructed by any power on earth.'' This is quoted constantly as a
confession. Read the sentence again. It is a conditional, and its point is that the United
States is not building them --- a denial of domestic manufacture, which in 1950 was true of
saucers and false of a great deal else.

\figwrap[r]{0.38}{Eisenhower delivering the farewell address of 17 January 1961 and its warning about the military--industrial complex.}{https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Eisenhower_farewell_address.jpg}{fig:ikefarewell}
\textbf{Eisenhower} disappeared from Palm Springs on the evening of 20 February 1954 and
reappeared the following morning; the press was told he had chipped a tooth and been taken to
a dentist. The dentist was named, appeared at a function that night, and confirmed it. This is
the basis of the persistent claim that Eisenhower met with extraterrestrials at Edwards Air
Force Base and signed a treaty. What Eisenhower actually did in office that bears on this book
is warn, in his 1961 farewell address (see Figure~\ref{fig:ikefarewell}), about the ``military-industrial complex'' --- a man who
had commanded the largest military operation in history telling his country that its arms
industry had acquired unaccountable influence. That is the real disclosure and nobody made a
movie about it.

\textbf{Kennedy} is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:jfk} and I will not preempt it.

\figwrap[l]{0.36}{Jimmy Carter, official portrait, 1977. The only US president to have filed a signed UFO sighting report.}{https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Official_Portrait_of_Jimmy_Carter_(1977).jpg}{fig:carter}
\textbf{Carter} is the case that matters. On 18 September 1973, while Governor of Georgia, he
completed and signed a sighting report form for the International UFO Bureau in Oklahoma City,
describing an object he and ten to twenty-five fellow Lions Club members had watched outside a
meeting hall in Leary, Georgia --- Carter's own accounts vary between ``about 10 or 12 other
people'' on the report form and ``about twenty of us'' in a later interview, and the larger
number may include bystanders who stepped outside when the light was pointed out. He gave the date as October 1969; the Lions Club's own records
put that Leary meeting on 6 January 1969, which is the first thing a careful reader should
notice --- a report written four years after the event, by a witness of unimpeachable honesty,
is already wrong about the month and the season. It was bright as the Moon, he wrote, blue then
red then blue, apparently approaching and receding, seen at about 7:15\,pm for ten to twelve
minutes, and ``we were then all sure that we had seen something unusual''.

Carter studied nuclear engineering at the United States Naval Academy. He was trained on
military protocols. He had a baseline understanding, above that of the general public, of what
technologies the military possessed. And according to his own words he explicitly stated that he
did not believe he had seen an alien spacecraft, and he did not believe that Earth had been
visited by extraterrestrials. He assumed it was an unidentified earthly phenomenon. If that is
the testimony of the witness it has to be taken as such. It is not the position of the
Ufologist to take sides.

The standard debunk is Venus, and Carter himself eventually accepted it --- though in a 2007
interview he pushed back, saying he was an amateur astronomer and knew what Venus looked like.
Venus was in the western sky and near maximum brilliance in early January 1969, and a bright
planet low in haze will flush red, appear to hang, and appear to drift as the observer's own
head moves --- the autokinetic effect of Section~\ref{sec:investigativetechniques}. But there
is a better candidate, and it is the reason I keep this case rather than filing it under
``planet''. Robert Sheaffer's reconstruction points to a barium-cloud release fired that evening
from Eglin Air Force Base in Florida by a team under the physicist Jere Justus: a rocket-borne
chemical payload, ignited at high altitude at 7:35\,pm Eastern, roughly 152\,km from Leary and
about 33$^\circ$ above the horizon in the west-south-west --- and barium clouds glow
\hi{blue-green first and then red} as the ionised barium and neutral strontium fluoresce, in
that order, for something on the order of ten minutes. Blue, then red, in the right place, at
the right time, for the right duration. That is not a planet and it is not a craft. It is a
physics experiment, which is the most Ufology answer imaginable.

What Carter did with it is the point, but it is not the only point. I am less interested in
what Carter concluded about the light than in whether the ten to twenty-five other people who
stood outside that hall with him can still be tracked down, if they are even alive any more.
A 1976 investigation found that most of the other witnesses either did not recall the event or
did not recall it as important; the only one who did, Fred Hart, remembered ``a blue light or
something --- like some kind of weather balloon they send out or something,'' and said it had
been ``pretty far back in my mind.'' One brief recollection, five decades old. That is the
witness record for a sighting that had a future President of the United States in the group,
and it is thin.

\actualq[case]{what Carter thought the light was}{where the rest of the witnesses went and whether any of them can still be found}

He campaigned in 1976 promising that ``if I become
President, I'll make every piece of information this country has about UFO sightings available
to the public and to scientists'', and he made the pledge this chapter will keep returning to:
\hi{``I'll never make fun of people who say they've seen unidentified objects in the sky.''} In
office, his science adviser Frank Press asked NASA in 1977 to take the subject on; NASA's
administrator Robert Frosch declined the following December, on the grounds that there was
nothing to study without new physical evidence. The release pledge died quietly against the
standing objection that the files were tangled up with ``defence implications'' --- the same
phrase you will meet again in Section~\ref{sec:blackbudgetfunding}. A president who had filed a
report of his own could not, or would not, open the drawer.

\textbf{Ford}, then House minority leader, wrote to the Armed Services Committee in March 1966
after the Michigan sightings demanding hearings, ``because I think we owe it to the people to
establish credibility regarding UFOs.'' He got them: the first congressional UFO hearing, April
1966, which produced the Condon Report of Section~\ref{sec:aftermathbluebook}. A future
president is therefore directly responsible for the document that closed the subject down.

\textbf{Reagan} is the most genuinely interesting. He described, on more than one occasion, an
incident in 1974 in a Cessna over California in which he and the pilot observed a light that
accelerated away at speed. And repeatedly, in front of the UN General Assembly in 1987 and
privately to Gorbachev at Geneva in 1985, he raised the idea that an alien threat would unite
humanity: ``how quickly our differences worldwide would vanish if we were facing an alien
threat from outside this world.'' Gorbachev confirmed the conversation happened. Reagan was a
man who thought in narratives and had watched \emph{The Day the Earth Stood Still}; he was
using an invasion as a rhetorical device for arms control. It worked, in the sense that Geneva
produced progress.

\textbf{Clinton} asked Webster Hubbell, his associate attorney general, to find out about UFOs
and about who killed Kennedy, and was, by Hubbell's account, stonewalled on both. John Podesta,
his chief of staff, has publicly and repeatedly advocated declassification.

\textbf{Obama} handled the question with jokes for eight years and then, in 2021, said plainly
that there is ``footage and records of objects in the skies that we don't know exactly what they
are.''

\begin{funfact}
The pattern across seventy years is not one of men guarding a secret. It is one of men
discovering that the executive branch is enormous, compartmented, and not obliged to tell the
president things he has no operational need to know. Clinton asked and did not get an answer.
That is not evidence of aliens. It is evidence about the structure of the American state, which
is the far more disturbing finding and the actual subject of this chapter.
\end{funfact}

\section{Black Budget Funding}\label{sec:blackbudgetfunding}

The classified portion of the US defence budget is real, publicly acknowledged, and enormous.
For recent fiscal years the requested classified figure has run in the vicinity of
\$80--100 billion annually, spread across the National Intelligence Program, the Military
Intelligence Program, and classified line items inside the services' research and procurement
accounts. This is not a conspiracy theory; it is printed in budget documents as aggregate
numbers with no detail, and Congress votes on it.

How it works matters more than the number. Funds are appropriated to unnamed programme
elements, sometimes with deliberately meaningless nicknames. \hi{Oversight is restricted to
specific committees, and within those, to cleared members and staff.} Special Access Programs
add a further layer, and ``unacknowledged'' SAPs are those whose existence is itself
classified --- meaning a cleared official may be legally obliged to deny that a programme
exists. That is the mechanism people are reaching for when they talk about a cover-up, and it
is genuine.

The historical track record proves it works. The U-2 flew in 1955 and was denied. The
SR-71's predecessor, the A-12, was built by roughly a few thousand people at Area 51 and stayed
secret for years. The F-117 flew operationally for six years, from 1983, before its existence
was acknowledged in 1988 --- an aircraft with a genuinely bizarre faceted shape, flying at
night over Nevada, in the exact period when Nevada UFO reports surged. The B-2, the Manhattan
Project with 130,\!000 employees, the CORONA satellite programme, the existence of the NSA
itself: all concealed successfully for years or decades.

\hi{So the claim ``the government can keep a secret this big'' is not naive.} It is demonstrated.

The counter-claim is more specific and it is where I part company with the disclosure movement.
Every one of those programmes eventually surfaced --- through congressional inquiry, through
budget forensics, through defectors, through the simple attrition of retirement and death.
Average time to exposure has been on the order of five to twenty-five years. A crash-retrieval
and reverse-engineering programme running continuously since 1947 would be, by a wide margin,
the longest-held technical secret in human history, held by an institution that could not keep
the Pentagon Papers, the Church Committee revelations, MKULTRA, COINTELPRO, Iran-Contra,
Abu Ghraib, or a quarter of a million diplomatic cables. The same organisation that leaks
constantly on everything else has, on this alone, achieved perfection.

\begin{funfact}
A specific piece of budget evidence does exist and deserves mention because it is real: the
Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program was funded at about \$22 million between 2007
and 2012, largely at the initiative of Senator Harry Reid, and administered substantially
through Robert Bigelow's aerospace company. Its existence was revealed in December 2017 by the
\emph{New York Times} and \emph{Politico}. So there was a secret government UFO programme, it
was concealed, and it came out --- in ten years, through journalism, exactly as the pattern
predicts. It also produced no craft, no bodies, and a set of videos discussed in Section~\ref{sec:uap}.
\end{funfact}

\section{Project Blue Book}\label{sec:bluebook}

Pacific Ocean hosts the explosion of the first US hydrogen bomb! The pill finally produced.
Jackie Gleason was the freshest new comedian, and the largest. There were 104 drive in theaters
in Canada (try and find one now). The year was 1952 and the United States was at the height of
the UFO craze following the recently released movie ``The Day the Earth Stood Still'' and the
apparent UFO crash just 5 years prior in the town of Roswell, New Mexico. By March of 1952,
President Harry Truman had commissioned a team of high-ranking military officers to undergo the
definitive UFO investigation. Following 2 prior investigations lead by the United States
government known simply as Project Sign and Project Grudge, this third installment in the
franchise was to be known as Project Blue Book.

So the United States Air Force ran three consecutive public UFO investigations, and their
progression tells you what the institution was actually doing.

\textbf{Project Sign}, 1948. Established after the Arnold sighting and the pilot deaths
associated with the Mantell crash. Sign's staff produced an ``Estimate of the Situation''
concluding that the objects were probably interplanetary. General Hoyt Vandenberg, Air Force
Chief of Staff, rejected the estimate for insufficient proof and it was destroyed. Note what
happened: the investigators concluded aliens, and the leadership said the evidence did not
support it. That is the opposite of the cover-up narrative and it is documented.

\textbf{Project Grudge}, 1949--1951. Named with more candour than intended. \hi{Grudge operated
from the conclusion that reports were misidentification and hoax, and its function was
debunking.} It was, by the assessment of Air Force personnel at the time, a public relations
exercise. Its files were poor.

\textbf{Project Blue Book}, 1952--1969. Seventeen years, 12,\!618 reported cases. Under Captain
Edward Ruppelt, who took over in 1952 and who coined the term ``UFO'' precisely to escape the
connotations of ``flying saucer'', Blue Book was briefly a serious operation: standardised
reporting forms, statistical analysis, a genuine attempt at method. Ruppelt left in 1953 and
the project declined into a complaints department, staffed at times by a single officer and a
secretary.

The final numbers: 701 cases, roughly 5.5\%, classified ``unidentified''. The remainder were
attributed to astronomical objects, aircraft, balloons, weather phenomena, hoaxes, and
insufficient data. J.~Allen Hynek was the project's scientific consultant for its entire
duration, and his trajectory is the most important thing about it: he arrived as a debunker,
spent twenty years reading reports, and left convinced the residue was real and had been
handled disgracefully. He was also personally responsible for the ``swamp gas'' explanation of
the 1966 Michigan sightings, which he regretted for the rest of his life and which taught him,
he said, what happens when you offer a plausible-sounding answer you have not verified.

\figwrap[l]{0.42}{A page of the declassified Robertson Panel report, January 1953, recommending a public ``debunking'' programme.}{https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Robertson_Panel_report_page.jpg}{fig:robertson}
The 1953 CIA-convened \textbf{Robertson Panel} is the single most consequential document here (see Figure~\ref{fig:robertson}).
A group of scientists including Luis Alvarez and Lloyd Berkner met for four days and concluded
that UFO reports posed no direct threat --- but that the \emph{volume} of reports posed a real
threat, by clogging military communications channels during a period when an actual Soviet
attack was the concern, and by leaving the public vulnerable to psychological warfare. Their
recommendation was a programme of ``debunking'' to reduce public interest, potentially using
mass media and, explicitly, Disney.

Read carefully, that is a genuine government conspiracy to manipulate public opinion about
UFOs, and it is fully documented in the panel's own declassified report. Its motive was not
hiding aliens. It was air defence bandwidth and Cold War psychology. The consequence, however,
was that official statements from 1953 onward were shaped by a policy of reducing interest
rather than a policy of finding out --- which poisoned the well permanently, and which is why
every denial issued since is reasonably suspected of being institutional rather than factual.
The government created the modern UFO conspiracy culture on purpose, by deciding that
reassurance mattered more than accuracy.

The United States was not the only government running a file. While Grudge was busy debunking
and Blue Book was still a year away, Canada quietly opened one of its own --- and it did not
sit inside the Air Force at all. \hi{Project Magnet was approved on 2 December 1950 and run out
of the Department of Transport}, the civilian department that licenses radio operators and keeps
the airwaves in order. Its author was Wilbert Brockhouse Smith, senior radio engineer for the
department's Broadcast and Measurements Section, who had gone to the 1950 North American
Regional Broadcasting Agreement conference to argue about AM frequency allocations and came home
having been told by the American physicist Robert Sarbacher that flying saucers were real and
that the American investigation of them was classified more highly than the hydrogen bomb.
Smith wrote a memo on 21 November 1950 asking permission to look into it. The memo stayed Top
Secret until 1969.

What makes Magnet worth putting beside Blue Book is that it was not a sightings-collection
exercise. Smith believed the earth's magnetic field was a usable source of energy, and his
stated aim was to work out whether it could be tapped for propulsion --- in other words, to
reverse-engineer the physics rather than catalogue the witnesses. He was joined by real people
with real posts: James Watt of the Defence Research Board, John Thompson of Transport, J.~Tuzo
Wilson of the University of Toronto, and G.~D. Garland of the Dominion Observatory. In 1952 Smith
was also appointed to \textbf{Project Second Storey}, a separate Canadian committee convened to
consider ``the UFO problem'', and he reported some of Magnet's findings to it. That autumn the
project put up a small instrumented observatory at Shirleys Bay outside Ottawa --- magnetometer,
gamma-ray detector, radio receiver, gravity meter, chart recorders --- which is, whatever you
make of the man, an automated multi-sensor station forty years before anyone used that phrase.

And here is where it goes wrong, which is exactly why it belongs in this chapter. Smith's own
reports, in June 1952 and again in 1953, concluded that the objects were of extraterrestrial
origin and operated on magnetic principles --- a conclusion his instruments could not carry. He
later said he was in telepathic contact with a group he called the Boys from Topside, and
material along those lines was published after his death. Official funding ended in mid-1954;
Smith kept the work going unfunded until he died of cancer in December 1962, reportedly having
dismantled an anti-gravity device on the grounds that the world was not ready for it. Blue Book
failed by refusing to conclude anything. Magnet failed by concluding far too much. \hi{Both
failures are the same failure: the instrument was never allowed to be the thing that decided.}

\begin{funfact}[Fun Fact: The Structure of This Book and the Man Who Ran Canada's UFO File Were Born in the Same Town]
\funfactlead{Wilbert Brockhouse Smith was born in Lethbridge, Alberta, in 1910.}
That is the same small prairie city where the structure and format of this book were built ---
the lecture series, the chapter order, the whole framework you are holding, drafted and taught
in Lethbridge before any of it looked like a book. Canada has had exactly one government UFO
investigation worth the name, and the engineer who wrote the memo that started it came from the
same place. The coincidence runs one layer deeper than geography: Magnet was run out of the
Department of Transport, and my own radio operator's licence was issued by Transport Canada ---
the same department, the same regulatory desk, three generations apart. It proves nothing. I
simply refuse to leave it out.
\end{funfact}

There is a second reason the Robertson Panel matters, and it is the reason this section cannot
stop at the Air Force. The panel is not an isolated document. It sits in a decade in which
several American agencies were, simultaneously and secretly, running programmes whose entire
purpose was to manipulate what people believed, remembered and reported --- and we know that
because the United States Senate eventually forced them into the open.

In 1975 the \textbf{Church Committee} --- formally the Senate Select Committee to Study
Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities, chaired by Frank Church of
Idaho --- spent a year taking testimony and published fourteen reports. It established, on the
record, that the CIA had plotted assassinations of foreign leaders, that the FBI had run
COINTELPRO against domestic political movements, that the NSA had been reading Americans'
cables under Project SHAMROCK, and that the CIA had opened mail and infiltrated the press. The
Rockefeller Commission and the Pike Committee covered adjacent ground in the same period. None
of this is contested and none of it is fringe: it is the published output of a Senate select
committee, and it is the single strongest piece of evidence available that agencies of the
United States government will lie to Congress and to the public for years at a stretch.

The committee's most relevant find, for our purposes, was \textbf{MKULTRA}. Authorised by
Director Allen Dulles in April 1953 --- three months after the Robertson Panel sat --- MKULTRA
ran for roughly a decade across some 149 subprojects at more than eighty institutions,
including universities and hospitals, and it was an attempt to find out whether human
perception, memory and behaviour could be altered on demand. Its methods included LSD
administered to unwitting subjects, hypnosis, sensory deprivation, sleep deprivation and
electroconvulsive treatment. It killed at least one man: Frank Olson, an Army biochemist dosed
without his knowledge in November 1953, fell from a New York hotel window nine days later.
Richard Helms ordered the files destroyed in 1973; the programme is known in the detail it is
known because a misfiled cache of about twenty thousand financial records escaped the shredder
and surfaced under a Freedom of Information request in 1977, prompting a further Senate hearing
under Ted Kennedy. In 1995 President Clinton apologised to the survivors of the related human
radiation experiments. \hi{An agency that gave LSD to unconsenting citizens and then burned the
evidence has forfeited any presumption of candour, and it is not paranoid to say so.}

So the ufologist's suspicion has a legitimate root, and I want to concede it in full before I
take anything back. The counter-argument that agencies would never conduct secret programmes
against their own public is simply false, and the Church Committee is why.

\actualq[lesson]{that the government would never run a secret programme against its own
citizens.}{that we know it did --- because a Senate committee documented it, which is precisely
the procedure that fringe claims never survive.}

And that is the turn. MKULTRA is not evidence for the UFO cover-up; it is the standard against
which the UFO cover-up claim fails. MKULTRA has budget lines, contract numbers, named
institutions, a body, surviving subjects, a Senate transcript and a presidential apology. It
was exposed by ordinary oversight machinery working slowly and badly, but working. The claim of
a retrieved-craft programme running since 1947 has, after eighty years, produced no equivalent
of any of those things. The comparison cuts the way sceptics say it does, not the way
conspiracy writers assume.

The distinction becomes obvious when a story borrows MKULTRA's clothes without borrowing its
paperwork. The \textbf{Montauk Project} --- treated at length in
Section~\ref{sec:montauk} --- alleges exactly this lineage: mind control, time travel and
interdimensional contact conducted in a secret laboratory beneath a Long Island radar station,
with the memories of the participants deliberately erased. Notice the structure. The erasure
clause is what removes the story from the category MKULTRA belongs to, because it explains in
advance why no document, no witness and no budget line will ever be produced. MKULTRA's records
were destroyed too --- and yet twenty thousand of them survived, along with the survivors
themselves, congressional testimony and a paper trail through eighty institutions. Real
programmes leak, because real programmes have accountants. \hi{The test is not whether a secret
programme is conceivable --- MKULTRA settles that --- but whether the specific claim has left
the residue that a real programme cannot avoid leaving.}

Which returns us to Blue Book with the question sharpened. The Air Force's seventeen years of
files, the Robertson Panel's debunking recommendation and MKULTRA's LSD subprojects all show
institutions behaving badly. What none of them shows is an institution successfully keeping a
discovery of that magnitude quiet. The documented conspiracies of the 1950s were conspiracies
of management and public relations, and every one of them was eventually written down by
somebody who kept a copy.

\subsection{The Aftermath of Blue Book}\label{sec:aftermathbluebook}

Blue Book was closed on 17 December 1969 on the recommendation of the \textbf{Condon Report} ---
formally the \emph{Scientific Study of Unidentified Flying Objects}, produced at the University
of Colorado under Edward Condon with Air Force funding and published in 1968; the Bantam
paperback edition that the public actually read runs to 965 pages.

Its conclusion: further study was unlikely to be productive, nothing had come out of
twenty-one years of investigation that advanced science, and there was no national security
implication.

My own verdict on it is shorter than the report deserves and exactly as short as it earned:
\hi{The Condon Report is a 965-page document of BULLSHIT!} And I want to be precise about why,
because the objection is not that Condon concluded UFOs are not alien spacecraft. He did not
even argue that. What the report attempts to establish is that zero scientific value can come
out of investigating the subject at all --- and that is NOT HOW SCIENCE WORKS. No study gets to
declare in advance that a question is barren; that is a prediction about future research, made
by people who wanted the funding to stop.

The Condon Report is a genuine scandal, and not for the reason usually claimed. The problem is
internal: Condon expressed contempt for the subject publicly before the study concluded, and a
project coordinator's memo --- the ``Low memo'', written by Robert Low before the project began
--- proposed that the study could be run so that ``the trick would be, I think, to describe the
project so that, to the public, it would appear a totally objective study but, to the
scientific community, would present the image of a group of nonbelievers.'' That memo leaked.
Two staff members were fired after circulating it. Hynek and James McDonald both attacked the
report's integrity.

It is worth asking why a university took this job at all, because the answer explains the
result. The Air Force had already been refused. After the Condon-style contract was floated,
several institutions with far stronger reputations in physics and astronomy looked at it and
declined, for the obvious reason: no physics department wanted its name in the newspapers
attached to flying saucers, whatever the finding turned out to be. The subject was understood
to be career damage in exchange for a grant. Boulder said yes anyway, and it said yes for a
reason that has nothing to do with curiosity about the phenomenon.

The contract was worth on the order of \$325,000 in 1966 dollars, and grew past half a million
before the project closed --- somewhere in the region of three to four million dollars in
present-day terms. A sum like that does not simply buy an investigation of the cases. It
arrives as a federal research contract, and a federal research contract carries indirect
costs: a share skimmed off the top for the institution itself, plus salaries for faculty time,
stipends for graduate students, secretarial and computing support, travel budgets, and the
standing overhead of laboratory and office space. In other words, the University of Colorado
was not being paid to be interested in unidentified flying objects. It was being paid, in the
practical sense that matters to a dean, to keep funding its own research programme, and the
saucers were the price of the cheque. Robert Low, who wrote the memo, was an administrator in
the graduate school, not an investigator, and his memo reads exactly like what it is --- a man
working out how to bank the money without the institution taking a reputational hit.

\hi{This is the structural point, and it recurs throughout this book: when the sponsor of a
study is also the party with an interest in its conclusion, and the host institution's real
motive is the funding rather than the question, the study's finding is decided by incentives
before any evidence is examined.} Nobody had to be bribed and nobody had to lie. A contract
was awarded to an institution that wanted the revenue and did not want the subject, and it
produced precisely the report such an arrangement produces.

And here is the detail that decides it: roughly 30\% of the cases examined \emph{in the body of
the report} were left unexplained by the report's own investigators, a higher unidentified rate
than Blue Book's. Condon's summary conclusions, written by Condon, do not reflect the contents
of the chapters they summarise. Anybody can verify this; the report is public. It is the
classic institutional manoeuvre --- commission a study, and write the executive summary
separately.

The American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics reviewed the report and disagreed with
its conclusion, stating that the study did not support the claim that nothing of scientific
value could come from further work. Nobody cared. Condon's summary was what the press printed,
Blue Book closed, official study ended, and the field was handed over entirely to amateurs for
the next forty-eight years, which is a substantial part of why this book has to spend its first
chapter teaching method.

\begin{funfact}
Official study did not actually end --- it just stopped being public. The 1969 termination
memo instructed that reports of national security significance should continue to be handled
through normal military channels, specifically JANAP 146 and Air Force Regulation 200-2, both
of which predated Blue Book and neither of which was rescinded. So the public office closed and
the classified reporting channel stayed open. Then in 2017 the Pentagon acknowledged AATIP, and
in 2022 Congress established the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office. Fifty-three years after
announcing there was nothing to investigate, the Department of Defense established a permanent
office to investigate it.
\end{funfact}

\subsubsection{The Grusch Testimony}\label{sec:grusch}

Which brings the story to 26 July 2023, and to the single most serious allegation ever made about
this subject under oath. David Grusch, then thirty-six, a former Air Force intelligence officer who
had served on the National Reconnaissance Office and had been the National Geospatial-Intelligence
Agency's representative to the Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force, told a subcommittee of the
House Oversight Committee that the United States operates a multi-decade programme to recover and
reverse-engineer craft of non-human origin, and that ``non-human biologics'' had been retrieved
from them. He first went public in June of that year through \emph{The Debrief} and in an interview
with the journalist Ross Coulthart, and he filed a complaint with the Intelligence Community
Inspector General alleging that he had been retaliated against for reporting the programme through
lawful channels.

I want to be extremely careful here, because both the enthusiastic and the dismissive readings of
this are wrong. What is genuinely new is procedural, and it is not small: a serving intelligence
officer with verified access and a clean record made these claims in open session, under oath,
under penalty of felony, and his Inspector General complaint was found urgent and credible ---
which is a statutory determination about whether a complaint warrants investigation, not a finding
that the underlying claim is true. Nothing remotely like that had happened in the previous
seventy-six years.

What is not new is the evidentiary situation. Grusch stated repeatedly that his knowledge was
derived from interviews with some forty other individuals whom he considered credible, that he had
not personally seen craft or bodies, and that the specifics --- names, locations, programme
designations --- could only be given in classified session. \hi{That is testimony about testimony,
which is the oldest structure in this book.} Section~\ref{sec:correlationvscausation} gave you the rule and I am not going to
suspend it for a witness I happen to find interesting. The Department of Defense denied having any
verifiable information about the programme he described; AARO's historical review, published in
March 2024, reported finding no evidence that any US programme had ever possessed extraterrestrial
technology, and attributed a portion of the recovered-craft folklore to genuine but conventional
classified projects and to a deliberate practice of using UFO cover stories to protect them.

So where does that leave us? With a named, credentialled, on-the-record witness alleging an
extraordinary thing, an official review denying it, and no physical evidence in the public domain
from either side. If the allegation is true, the classification structure is the reason we cannot
check it. If it is false, the classification structure is also the reason we cannot check it. That
symmetry is uncomfortable and I am not going to resolve it for you. What I will say is that the
correct posture towards Grusch is neither belief nor mockery but the one this whole course argues
for: hold the claim open, insist that the resolution come through documents and materials rather
than through further interviews, and notice which side of the argument is asking you to accept a
chain of unnamed sources.

\section{The JFK Assassination}\label{sec:jfk}

The UFO connection to the Kennedy assassination rests on two documents, and I am going to be
blunt about them because this is where the field embarrasses itself most reliably.

The first is a memorandum allegedly dated 12 November 1963, from Kennedy to the Director of
Central Intelligence, requesting a review of UFO intelligence files and referencing
cooperation with the Soviet Union on space activities. The second is a purported CIA document
known in the community as the ``burned memo'', containing the line about ``Lancer'' --- the
Secret Service codename for Kennedy --- being a problem.

The November 1963 memo is real, and it says something less interesting than advertised: it is
a National Security Action Memorandum directing cooperation with the USSR on space matters,
which is consistent with Kennedy's September 1963 UN speech proposing a joint Moon mission.
That proposal is genuinely remarkable and genuinely relevant to Cold War history. It is not
about aliens. The associated ``UFO files'' memo has never been authenticated, exists in
photocopy, and comes through the same channels as the material in Section~\ref{sec:mj12}.

The burned memo is not authenticated by anybody, was supplied by a single anonymous source to
one researcher, and shows physical damage consistent with someone deliberately burning a
photocopy.

Here is why this matters beyond the specific claim. The Kennedy assassination has genuine
unresolved features: the acoustic evidence, the House Select Committee's 1979 conclusion of
probable conspiracy, the documented CIA and FBI withholding of information from the Warren
Commission, the Mafia and anti-Castro connections, and the fact that thousands of records
remained sealed into the 2020s. There is a serious body of work there. Attaching UFOs to it
does not strengthen the UFO case; it weakens the assassination case, by giving anybody who
wants to dismiss all of it an easy reason to.

This is the mechanism I called conspiracy accretion in Section~\ref{sec:confirmationbias}. A theory that explains
everything explains nothing, and a movement that adopts every available mystery as a subsidiary
of its own is not investigating --- it is collecting.

\section{MAJESTIC 12}\label{sec:mj12}

In December 1984 a roll of undeveloped 35mm film arrived in the mail at the home of Jaime
Shandera, a television producer working with researcher William Moore. Developed, it contained
photographs of an eight-page briefing document dated 18 November 1952, prepared for
President-elect Eisenhower, describing ``Operation Majestic 12'' --- a group of twelve
scientists and officials overseeing the recovery of crashed extraterrestrial craft and bodies at
Roswell and elsewhere.

The named members are real and impressive: Vannevar Bush, James Forrestal, Roscoe Hillenkoetter,
Nathan Twining, Hoyt Vandenberg, Detlev Bronk, Donald Menzel and others --- exactly the group
you would assemble for such a task, which is either evidence of authenticity or evidence that
the forger did their homework.

The documents are forgeries, and the case against them is technical and decisive:

\textbf{Date format.} The documents use ``18 November, 1952'' --- with a comma after the month
in a military-style date. This is wrong, and consistently wrong, for the period and the
agencies involved. Correct usage is ``18 November 1952''. The comma is a distinctively civilian
American habit.

\textbf{Typeface.} Analysis identified the type as consistent with a Smith-Corona machine and,
more damagingly, the documents' formatting conventions do not match verified Truman-era
Executive Office documents.

\textbf{The Truman-Forrestal memo.} An attached memo bears Truman's signature, and that
signature is a pixel-for-pixel match --- identical size and shape --- to Truman's signature on
a genuine, publicly available 1 October 1947 letter to Vannevar Bush. Signatures are never
identical. It was photocopied and pasted.

\textbf{Classification markings.} The documents use ``TOP SECRET / MAJIC EYES ONLY'', a
classification and control marking that appears nowhere in the vast declassified record of the
period, and their handling instructions are inconsistent with the security manuals in force.

\textbf{Provenance.} Anonymous film in the mail. There is no chain of custody at all.

The FBI investigated and stamped its copy ``BOGUS''. The Air Force Office of Special
Investigations concluded the same. And in the 1980s AFOSI is documented --- in the Paul
Bennewitz affair --- to have deliberately fed fabricated UFO material to researchers as a
counterintelligence exercise, in Bennewitz's case with results that destroyed the man's mental
health. Richard Doty, an AFOSI agent, has admitted participating in disinformation. William
Moore has admitted cooperating with intelligence contacts.

\begin{funfact}
The most consequential thing about MJ-12 is not whether it is fake --- it is fake --- but that
it worked. Almost the entire modern architecture of UFO conspiracy belief descends from these
eight pages: the crash retrieval programme, the secret oversight committee, the ``EBE''
vocabulary of Section~\ref{sec:extraterrestrialbiologic}, the treaty narrative, the underground facilities of
Chapter~\ref{ch:underground}. A document rejected by the FBI in 1988 is still the load-bearing
wall of a belief system followed by millions. That is worth more study than the document.
\end{funfact}

\subsection{Where the Twelve Came From}\label{sec:mj12origin}

The forgery's cleverest feature is its membership list. Whoever assembled those eight pages did
not invent twelve names; they went shopping in the 1947 national-security establishment and took
twelve men who were genuinely there, genuinely cleared, and in several cases genuinely mixed up
with the flying-disc problem. That is why the document survives every debunking. Readers look at
the list, recognise that these are exactly the people who \emph{would} have been told, and
conclude that the list must be the leak. The correct conclusion is the opposite and far more
uncomfortable: the list is accurate because the secrecy machine it describes was real. There was
no Majestic~12. There was something better organised than Majestic~12, and it was not secret at
all --- it was published, funded, and testified to before Congress.

So let us do what the believers and the debunkers both skip. Instead of arguing about the
document, let us look at the twelve men. Not as saucer custodians: as who they actually were, and
what they actually controlled. Every one of them held power over information. Between them they
ran the atomic-weapons stockpile, the first peacetime intelligence service, the Air Force, the
nation's science budget, its aeronautical research, its academies, its psychological-warfare
planning and, in one case, its codebreaking consultancy. \hi{The Men in Black are real. They wore
suits, they had names, they signed things, and their job was deciding what the public would be
allowed to know.} That is the story the forgery is standing on top of, and it is bigger than the
forgery.

\subsection{The Death of James Forrestal}\label{sec:forrestal}

A little before two o'clock on the morning of 22 May 1949, James Forrestal --- seven weeks out of
office as the first Secretary of Defense of the United States, a patient on the sixteenth floor of
the Bethesda Naval Hospital --- went out of a window across the corridor from his room and fell to
the roof of the third-floor passageway. He was fifty-seven. The story the world was given, within
hours, was tidy: a depressed man, copying out the chorus from Sophocles' \emph{Ajax}, stopped in
the middle of the word ``nightingale'' and killed himself.

The UFO literature has built a cathedral on that window --- Forrestal silenced, Forrestal thrown,
Forrestal the man who knew. I want to be exact here, because the exactness is the point.
\hi{There is no evidence whatsoever that Forrestal's death had anything to do with UFOs.} None.
Not a document, not a witness, not a line in a file. Every version of that claim traces back to
the forged MJ-12 papers or to authors quoting them.

And yet. The official investigation into the death of the most senior defence official in the
United States was conducted by a naval board of inquiry under Admiral Morton Willcutts, and its
report was not released to the public until 2004 --- fifty-five years --- when a researcher
obtained it and it was placed in the Mudd Manuscript Library at Princeton. Once you read the
report rather than the newspapers, the tidy story stops being tidy. Six things in that record do
not sit properly.

\textbf{One: the timeline contradicts the story.} The hospital corpsman assigned to watch
Forrestal testified that at about a quarter to two he looked in and Forrestal appeared to be
asleep, then left the room briefly. That is minutes before the fall. A man asleep is not a man
halfway through transcribing a Greek chorus.

\textbf{Two: nobody is named as having found the transcription.} The half-copied Sophocles is the
single most quoted detail of the whole affair, the emotional proof of suicide --- and the board's
record never establishes who found the book and the paper, or when, or where in the room. The
most famous piece of evidence in the case has no witness attached to it.

\textbf{Three: the sash.} Forrestal's dressing-gown cord was found knotted tightly around his
neck. It did not hold him; it did not break his fall; it was simply there, tied, after a
sixteen-storey drop. No official account has ever explained it, and the explanations offered ---
a failed hanging attempt, an attempt to lower himself --- are reconstructions, not findings.

\textbf{Four: the broken glass.} Photographs taken in the room show broken glass on the bed,
inside the room. Glass from a window a man has gone out of falls outward and downward. Glass on
the bedding says something happened in the room before he went through it.

\textbf{Five: the wrong window.} He did not go out of his own window. He went out of a small
kitchen across the hall --- a room he had to leave his own to reach, on a floor where he was
supposed to be under observation, in the few minutes his watch was elsewhere.

\textbf{Six: the board never said suicide.} This is the one that should stop every reader. The
Willcutts board concluded that Forrestal died of injuries from the fall. It did not find that he
took his own life. It made no finding of suicide at all --- and then the whole report was withheld
for fifty-five years, during which every newspaper, encyclopaedia and biography reported the
suicide as settled fact.

\pullquote[Willcutts Report, finding of fact, 1949]{``The death was caused by injuries, multiple,
extreme, received incident to a fall from a high point.'' The board's finding names the fall. It
names no cause for the fall.}

I am not going to tell you what happened in that kitchen, because I do not know and neither does
anybody else living. I will tell you what the record supports. A man in a locked psychiatric ward,
with a Marine guard on the floor and a corpsman assigned to him, under observation because the
United States government was afraid he would do exactly this, was left alone long enough to walk
out of his room, cross a corridor, and go through a window --- and the official investigation into
how that happened was classified for longer than most of the people involved went on living.
The suicide may well be the truth. It is not, on this record, a finding. It is an assumption that
was allowed to harden while the evidence stayed in a safe.

That is the honest shape of the Forrestal case, and it is also the shape of this entire chapter.
The forged document tells you twelve men hid an alien. The real document tells you that when the
first Secretary of Defense of the United States died in a naval hospital, the government's own
inquiry could not, or would not, say why --- and kept its silence for fifty-five years.
\hi{You do not need a conspiracy to lose the truth. Secrecy and a filing cabinet will do it.}

\section{The Men in Black}\label{sec:menblack}

The Men in Black arrived in the literature through Albert Bender, who ran the International
Flying Saucer Bureau and shut it down abruptly in 1953, telling subscribers he had been visited
by three men in dark suits who told him to stop. Gray Barker turned this into
\emph{They Knew Too Much About Flying Saucers} in 1956, and John Keel expanded the mythology in
the 1970s. Bender himself later published a different account --- \emph{Flying Saucers and the
Three Men} (1962) --- describing telepathic entities in a mountain base in Antarctica, which
tends not to get quoted.

The reported profile is consistent: two or three men, black suits of slightly wrong cut, black
cars of an old model in impossibly new condition, pale or oddly tinted skin, monotone or
stilted speech, unfamiliarity with ordinary objects and idioms, knowledge of things they should
not know, and either a warning to stop talking or a strange interrogation. They arrive shortly
after a sighting and before it is public.

The mundane explanations account for most of it and they are not boring.

Actual government agents did visit UFO witnesses, routinely, and this is documented. Air Force
investigators under AFR 200-2 interviewed witnesses; so did the FBI, and so did the OSI. In the
1950s and 1960s a federal agent's uniform was, functionally, a dark suit, a hat, and a government sedan (see Figure~\ref{fig:bluebookint}) --- and agents were frequently instructed to discourage witnesses from talking
to the press, which was Robertson Panel policy from 1953. An anxious witness visited by two men
in dark suits with government credentials who told them not to discuss the matter publicly has
not had a paranormal experience. They have had an accurate one.

Add to that: journalists, insurance adjusters, private researchers with self-important manners,
Mormon missionaries, and outright pranksters. And the ``uncanny'' details --- stilted speech,
wrong idioms, unfamiliarity with a ballpoint pen --- are exactly what memory does to a
frightening encounter recalled years later after exposure to the folklore, which is
Section~\ref{sec:hypnosis} operating without a hypnotist.

The cultural trajectory is the joke. By 1997 the MIB were a Will Smith comedy franchise, and
the modern reported cases have been contaminated by it beyond recovery.

\section{The Illuminati}\label{sec:illuminati}

The Bavarian Illuminati was founded on 1 May 1776 by Adam Weishaupt, a professor of canon law
at Ingolstadt. It was a real organisation with real goals: Enlightenment rationalism, opposition
to clerical control of education, and reform of the Bavarian state. It recruited within Masonic
lodges, reached perhaps 2,\!000 members at its peak, and was suppressed by the Elector of
Bavaria's edicts of 1784 and 1785. Weishaupt fled and died in obscurity in 1830. That is the
history, and it is not in dispute.

The conspiracy version was manufactured almost immediately, by two authors reacting to the
French Revolution: Augustin Barruel in \emph{M\'emoires pour servir \`a l'histoire du
jacobinisme} (1797) and John Robison in \emph{Proofs of a Conspiracy} (1798). Both argued the
Revolution was engineered by surviving Illuminati. This was politically useful to people who
preferred not to consider that the French had revolted over bread and taxes.

The modern version accumulated by the same process I described in Section~\ref{sec:confirmationbias}: absorb Masonic
symbolism, absorb the \emph{Protocols of the Elders of Zion} (a Tsarist secret police forgery
of around 1903, demonstrated as such by \emph{The Times} in 1921, and the direct ancestor of
most modern global-conspiracy structure), absorb central banking, absorb the eye on the dollar
bill --- which is the Eye of Providence, a common eighteenth-century Christian motif, on a
Great Seal designed in 1782 by people who were not shy about explaining their choices --- and
finally, in the 1990s, absorb aliens.

I want to name the specific reason I refuse to treat this material as a harmless parlour game.
The structure of the Illuminati narrative --- a small hidden group of financiers manipulating
nations, media, and money --- is the structure of antisemitic conspiracy theory, because that is
literally where the modern version came from. The \emph{Protocols} were antisemitic propaganda
that contributed materially to the Holocaust. When a modern theory says ``Illuminati'' or
``globalist bankers'' in the slot where the \emph{Protocols} said something else, the argument
has not changed; only the label has. David Icke's reptilian version, which I will address in
Chapter~\ref{ch:underground}, maps onto this lineage in a way its adherents mostly do not
realise and should.

\begin{funfact}
The genuinely useful lesson here is about how power actually operates, because it is not
hidden. Lobbying disclosures, campaign finance records, corporate board interlocks, the
revolving door between regulators and the industries they regulate, Bilderberg and Davos
attendee lists --- all public, all documented, all considerably more influential than any
secret society. The people who run things do not need to hide. They file paperwork. Any evening
spent reading lobbying registrations will teach you more about who controls the world than the
entire Illuminati literature, and it has the advantage of being true.
\end{funfact}

\section{The Phoenix Lights}\label{sec:phoenixlights}

I have kept this case for the end of the chapter because it is the one that closes the circle.
The chapter opened with a governor who filed an honest UFO report and was mocked for it. It ends
with a governor who mocked the witnesses, on camera, in front of the world's press --- and who
waited ten years to admit that he had seen the thing too.

On the evening of Thursday 13 March 1997, thousands of people across Arizona and into Nevada and
Sonora watched lights move over their heads. Calls came into Luke Air Force Base, into the
sheriff's offices, into the newspapers, into the National UFO Reporting Center in numbers that
nobody in Phoenix had seen before. What they were describing was not one event, and this is the
single most important fact about the Phoenix Lights, routinely lost in the retelling.

\textbf{The first event, roughly 8:15 to 8:45\,pm}, was a formation of lights moving slowly from
the north-west down the state --- Henderson, Nevada, then Paulden, Prescott Valley, Dewey,
Phoenix, Tucson. Many witnesses reported not separate lights but a single enormous dark
triangular mass occluding the stars between them, passing overhead in silence. Mitch Stanley, a
Phoenix amateur astronomer, put a 10-inch Dobsonian telescope on the formation as it went over
and saw, in his words, ``aircraft --- distinct, separate, with wings, flying in V formation.''

\figwrap[r]{0.42}{A-10 Thunderbolt IIs of the Maryland Air National Guard in formation. Aircraft of this type, and the LUU-2B flares they drop, are the conventional explanation for both halves of the Phoenix Lights.}{https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:175th_Wing_-_A-10_Thunderbolt_IIs_Warfield_Air_National_Guard_Base_Maryland.jpg}{fig:a10phx}
\textbf{The second event, at about 10:00\,pm}, is the one everybody has actually seen, because it
was filmed. A row of brilliant lights hung above the southwestern horizon, seen from Phoenix
over the Estrella mountain range, appearing one after another and then fading one after another
over several minutes. This is the footage that runs on television. The retired USAF pilot and
astronomer James McGaha identified it early and identified it correctly: LUU-2B illumination
flares, dropped by A-10 Thunderbolt IIs of the 104th Fighter Squadron, Maryland Air National
Guard, on a night training exercise over the Barry M.\ Goldwater Range south-west of the city
(see Figure~\ref{fig:a10phx}). The lights sat in a line because the aircraft flew a line; they
appeared and vanished in sequence because flares ignite in sequence and burn out in sequence;
they seemed to sink behind a solid black edge because the Estrellas were in front of them. The
Maryland ANG confirmed the sortie. A 1998 test drop reproduced the effect on video. This half of
the case is closed, and anyone who tells you otherwise is either uninformed or selling
something.

\actualq[case]{a single mile-wide craft over Phoenix at ten o'clock at night}{why an entirely
conventional formation flight at 8:30 and an entirely conventional flare drop at 10:00 produced
the most reported UFO event in American history --- and why the state's own governor lied about
what he had seen.}

\figwrap[l]{0.34}{Fife Symington III, Governor of Arizona 1991--1997. In June 1997 he staged the ``alien'' press conference; in 2007 he told James Fox he had seen the craft himself.}{https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fife_Symington_by_Gage_Skidmore.jpg}{fig:symington}
Which brings us to Fife Symington III. The Governor of Arizona was under enormous public
pressure to say something, and in June 1997 he called a press conference to announce that the
mystery had been solved and that the culprit was in custody. In walked a figure in a silver suit
and a grey rubber alien mask, flanked by two deputies, handcuffed. The room laughed. It played on
the news. Every person in Arizona who had rung the sheriff's office in March was, in that
moment, the punchline.

The man in the mask was Jay Heiler, Symington's own chief of staff, chosen because at six feet
four he looked absurd. Symington has since said the stunt was meant kindly --- to defuse
hysteria, to lower the temperature, to let people laugh instead of panic. Whatever the
intention, the effect was the one this book keeps documenting: it taught several thousand people
that reporting what you see gets you humiliated by your own government.

And Symington was one of them. In 2007, ten years on, he sat down with the film-maker James Fox
and said so on the record, and repeated it on CNN and in the \emph{Daily Courier}: he had driven
out that night, seen an enormous craft pass overhead in silence, and had no idea what it was.
``I'm a pilot and I know just about every machine that flies,'' he said. ``It was bigger than
anything that I've ever seen. It remains a great mystery. Other people saw it, responsible
people. I don't know why people would ridicule it.'' He called it ``otherworldly''.

\pullquote[Fife Symington III, 2007]{``I did not want to panic the public. But I also saw
something that night that I could not explain, and I never told anyone about it except my
wife.''}

Why the silence? Symington's own answer is worth more than any theory about it. He was, in 1997,
a sitting governor facing federal bank-fraud charges over his property business --- charges that
convicted him that September and forced his resignation, and were later overturned on appeal
before a pardon closed the matter. A man in that position does not volunteer that he has seen a
spacecraft. \hi{The cost of testifying was, for him personally, career annihilation} --- so he
told his wife and nobody else, and to manage the story publicly he made himself the loudest
sceptic in the state.

I want to be careful about what this does and does not prove. It does \emph{not} prove there was
a craft. Symington was a witness with the same eyes and the same visual system as everybody
else, watching what Mitch Stanley resolved into aeroplanes through a telescope; being a pilot
makes you better at recognising aircraft in daylight at known ranges and does very little for
you when the target is unlit lights at night at unknown distance --- pilots are, on the record,
among the worst-calibrated witnesses in aviation for exactly that reason. My own assessment is
that both halves of 13 March 1997 were military aircraft, and that the Governor of Arizona
misjudged a formation flight in the dark just as thousands of his constituents did.

What it proves is something about the machinery. \hi{The most powerful man in the state was
afraid to file the report he had mocked others for filing.} If that is the incentive structure
facing a governor, consider the incentive structure facing an airline captain with a medical
certificate to protect, or a serving officer with a security clearance, or a nurse coming off
shift. Every chapter of this book that complains about the thinness of the data is complaining,
in the end, about this.

\begin{funfact}
The two governors bracket the whole problem. Jimmy Carter saw a light he could not identify,
wrote it down, signed it, put his name and address on the form, later accepted a mundane
explanation, and promised never to ridicule anybody who did the same. Fife Symington saw
something he could not identify, said nothing for a decade, and in the interval put a man in a
rubber mask in handcuffs in front of the cameras. One of those men made the data better. The
other is the reason we have so little of it.
\end{funfact}
