\section{The Solar System}\label{sec:solarsystem}

Before we can argue about who else is out there, we need to be honest about where \emph{here}
is. Almost every claim in this chapter --- and a great many of the claims in the rest of the
book --- depends on a sense of distance, and most people, including most of my students on the
first day, do not have one. So this section and the next are the plumbing. Neither contains a
single UFO. Both are prerequisites for reading the ones that follow without being fooled.

Start with the local arrangement. One star, eight planets, several dwarf planets, a few
hundred known moons and an enormous quantity of debris, all of it orbiting in roughly the same
plane because it condensed out of the same rotating disc of gas about four and a half billion
years ago. The inner four planets are small and rocky because the young Sun boiled the
volatiles out of the inner disc; the outer four are large and gaseous because out there the
volatiles stayed put. That is the whole architecture, and it is not a coincidence --- it is a
fingerprint of the process that produced us, and we now see the same fingerprint around other
stars.

The unit that makes this tractable is the \term{astronomical unit}: one AU is the mean
Earth\ndash{}Sun distance, about 150 million kilometres. Light covers it in a little over
eight minutes.

The fourth column is not used anywhere in this book, and I want to be exact about what
it is before anybody builds on it. These are the figures quoted in the ``planetary tone''
literature: an orbital or rotational period, which is a cycle measured in days or years,
repeatedly doubled until it lands in the range a human ear can hear, a procedure introduced
by the Swiss mathematician and musicologist Hans Cousto in 1978. Earth is the exception on
the list. Its 7.83~Hz is the Schumann resonance, the fundamental standing wave in the cavity
between the ground and the ionosphere, and unlike the others it is a real electromagnetic
oscillation that instruments measure rather than a number produced by octave arithmetic.
Nothing in the vacuum between here and Mercury is vibrating at 141.27~Hz to be heard. I
tabulate the column because these figures are the entry point to a much larger conversation
about mysticism, resonance and correspondence that I intend to take up properly in a second
volume, and it is better to set them down accurately here, with their provenance stated,
than to paraphrase them later from memory.

Read the right-hand column rather than the middle one. Distances in kilometres are numbers you
cannot feel; travel times are not. The fastest object we have ever launched has been running
for nearly fifty years and is still less than a day of light away. \hi{Nothing human-built has
yet reached even one thousandth of the distance to the nearest star.}

The places worth naming for our purposes are the ones where the question of life is not
absurd. Mars, which had standing water and a thicker atmosphere and lost both. Europa and
Ganymede at Jupiter, and Enceladus and Titan at Saturn, where the interesting real estate is
under the ice rather than on it --- Enceladus is actively venting salt water and organic
molecules into space through cracks at its south pole, which is as close to a free sample as
the solar system is ever likely to offer. Note what all of these have in common: they are
candidates for \emph{microbial} life, not for engineers. Nobody serious is proposing that the
craft in Chapter~\ref{ch:roswell} came from Enceladus.

\subsection*{Why the big ones are at the back}

Students almost always ask the wrong version of this. They want to know whether other stars
have planets, which is now a settled question --- we have confirmed several thousand --- when
the interesting question is why ours are arranged in the order they are.

\actualq{do other solar systems have planets?}{why are the gaseous planets the ones furthest
out?}

The answer is temperature, and it was fixed before any of the planets existed. The collapsing
cloud that became the Sun left behind a hot, rotating disc of gas and dust, and that disc had a
thermal gradient: scorching near the middle, bitter at the rim. Somewhere between the present
orbits of Mars and Jupiter lay the \term{frost line}, the radius at which water could survive
as ice instead of vapour. Inside it, only substances with high melting points --- iron,
nickel, silicates --- could condense into solid grains, and those make up a tiny fraction of
the available material. Outside it, water, ammonia and methane froze out as well, which
multiplied the amount of solid matter available to build with several times over.

That is the whole trick. Beyond the frost line there was simply more to work with, so the
seed bodies out there grew faster and got bigger. Once a growing core passed roughly ten Earth
masses it could hold onto hydrogen and helium by gravity alone, and having started, it kept
going: the more gas it captured the harder it pulled, and the disc was overwhelmingly hydrogen
and helium. Jupiter and Saturn ran away with that process and became gas giants. Uranus and
Neptune started later and further out, where the disc was thinner and the orbits longer, and
ran out of gas before they finished --- which is why they are ice giants, mostly water,
ammonia and methane over a rocky core, rather than scaled-down Jupiters. The inner four never
had the chance. They were assembled from rock and metal only, and whatever hydrogen they did
capture was stripped away by the young Sun's radiation and stellar wind.

One caveat, because the tidy story has been revised in my lifetime. The planets did not
necessarily form where we now find them. The current models have the giants migrating
substantially --- Jupiter inwards and then out again, Uranus and Neptune possibly swapping
places --- shepherded by their gravitational grip on the remaining disc, and it is that
reshuffle which is thought to have scattered the asteroid belt and delivered the late
bombardment recorded all over the inner solar system. So the order is a fingerprint of
chemistry, but the spacing carries a record of a violent adolescence.

Which brings us back to life, and to the distinction that matters for everything that follows.
There are two sorts of real estate here. There are surfaces --- the rocky inner worlds, where
liquid water needs an atmosphere and a decent orbit to persist, and where only Earth currently
manages it. And there are the buried oceans of the outer system, kept liquid not by sunlight
but by tidal flexing beneath tens of kilometres of ice, at Europa, at Ganymede, at Enceladus.
If you are hunting for biology, the second category is far larger than the first, and it is
where most of the liquid water in the solar system actually is. If you are hunting for anyone
capable of building a vehicle, keep it in mind that a species under permanent ice has no sky
to look at and no obvious reason to invent metallurgy. The nine pages that follow are the
neighbourhood, in order, at a glance.

\begin{funfact}
The solar system is mostly nothing. If you shrank the Sun to the size of a beach ball two
metres across, Earth would be a peppercorn twenty-one metres away, Neptune would be a small
marble two thirds of a kilometre out, and the nearest other beach ball would be about five and
a half thousand kilometres away --- roughly Lethbridge to London. Every scale model you have
ever seen in a museum or a textbook, including the ones in this book, is lying to you about the
spacing, because an honest one is almost entirely empty page.
\end{funfact}

\subsubsection*{Change the Planet, or Change the Passenger?}

Read the nine biosphere lines above in sequence and one thing becomes obvious: rocky, rocky,
rocky/water, gaseous, gaseous. Every serious settlement plan we have written so far assumes we fix
the mismatch from the outside. Warm Mars up, thicken the air, melt the caps, seed the soil,
spend centuries and a planet's worth of industry making one world resemble the only world we
already have. Terraforming is engineering on a scale nobody has ever attempted, aimed at leaving
the human body exactly as it is.

The other direction is far cheaper and almost never discussed in polite company: change the
passenger. If Mars is cold, thin-aired, and drenched in radiation, then the question is not how
many millennia it takes to give Mars an atmosphere, but which terrestrial animals already solved
each of those problems and what their DNA would do inside a human genome. Freeze tolerance, high-
altitude oxygen efficiency, radiation-resistant cell repair, bone density that does not evaporate
at 0.38\,g --- these are not hypothetical traits. They are walking around on this planet right
now, distributed across species that never needed to leave it.

Follow that logic outward and it does not stop at Mars. Two of the nine worlds above are gaseous
and two more are rocky cores wrapped in gas. Breathing there is not an air-supply problem, it is a
medium problem --- and the animals that solved extraction-from-a-dense-medium are oceanic. Gills
are a filter for a fluid. Jupiter's cloud decks are a fluid. The step after adapting a human to
thin, cold, high-radiation rock is adapting one to a thick, chemically hostile soup, and the
template for that lives in the sea, not in a factory.

Whichever direction you find more comfortable, notice that the theological objection cuts both
ways. If altering the human body is presumption against God's plan, then so is altering an entire
planet He made; both are the same act at different scales, and only one of them is usually called
sacrilege. A reader who believes we were made deliberately should find it at least worth
considering that a body designed to be adapted is a more generous piece of design than a body
designed for one address.

So the real question is not \emph{whether} but \emph{what}. What modification. Which species
donates it. How much of it can a genome absorb before the result is no longer human in any sense
the word survives. And --- the part every plan skips --- what would such a person actually look
like standing on the surface of Mars, or floating in the clouds of Jupiter? We have no honest
picture of that. Keep it in mind as we move outward from the Solar System, because if a
civilisation older than ours ever asked itself this question, the answer is what would step off
the craft.

\section{Cosmology}\label{sec:cosmology}

One step out, and the units have to change again. A \term{light year} is the distance light
covers in a year, about 9.5 trillion kilometres, or roughly 63,\!000 AU. Proxima Centauri, the
nearest star to the Sun, is 4.24 light years away. In the beach-ball model above, that was the
trip from Alberta to England.

Our galaxy, the Milky Way, is a barred spiral roughly 100,\!000 light years across containing
somewhere between one and four hundred billion stars. We sit about 26,\!000 light years from
the centre, in a minor spiral arm, on a 230-million-year orbit. Beyond it, the observable
universe contains on the order of two trillion galaxies, and it has been expanding for 13.8
billion years from a hot dense state --- the \term{Big Bang}, which was not an explosion in
space but an expansion of it. The evidence for that is not a matter of taste: the recession of
distant galaxies, the cosmic microwave background at 2.7 kelvin in every direction, and the
observed ratio of hydrogen to helium all point the same way, and they were three independent
measurements.

Two consequences matter for everything that follows in this book.

The first is that looking out is looking back. Light has a finite speed, so every observation
is a historical document. The Andromeda galaxy as you see it tonight is two and a half million
years old. \hi{There is no such thing as an image of the universe as it is now}, and any claim
that depends on knowing what a distant system is doing at this moment is not a claim about
astronomy.

The second is the one that does the real work in Section~\ref{sec:fermi}. Interstellar
distance is not a large version of interplanetary distance; it is a different problem. Voyager
1, at its present speed, would need about 73,\!000 years to cover 4.24 light years. Get to one
tenth of the speed of light --- which is roughly ten thousand times faster than anything we
have built, and would require energies comparable to a large country's annual output --- and
the trip is still forty-two years one way, with a communications lag of over four years in
each direction. This is why the phrase ``they could not possibly get here'' and the phrase
``they are obviously here'' are both stated with more confidence than either deserves. The
first assumes our propulsion is the ceiling. The second assumes the ceiling has been broken by
someone, somewhere, without saying how.

My own position is more fantastic than both. I subscribe to the extra-tempestrial view ---
that what we are dealing with are time travellers, not space travellers --- as the most
plausible answer for what is happening. The distance problem vanishes entirely if the visitors
are not crossing space at all, and Chapter~\ref{ch:time} lays out the physics and the
cases that make this more than a daydream. \hi{The distances do not make visitation impossible;
they make it expensive, and expense is a testable claim.} Anything crossing that gap needs a
power source, a means of shedding waste heat, and a trajectory --- all three of which leave
physical traces of a kind our instruments could in principle detect. That is a far more useful
line of enquiry than arguing about whether it can be done, and it is the line the rest of this
chapter takes.

I will say, because honesty requires it, that the extra-tempestrial framing is not the
outer edge of what I think. There are positions I hold that I cannot yet argue in this volume
because the ground has not been laid. By the end of Volume~2 the reader will have spent half
a book breaking down what consciousness actually is, and some of what follows from that
breakdown is stranger still. That argument is not ready. This one is.

\begin{funfact}
The cosmic microwave background is about one per cent of the static an old analogue television
picked up on an empty channel. Penzias and Wilson found it in 1964 while trying to eliminate
noise from a radio antenna, assumed for months that the problem was pigeon droppings in the
horn, cleaned the pigeons out, and still had the signal. It won them a Nobel Prize. There is a
lesson in that for Ufology, and it is not the one usually drawn: the anomaly was real, but it
took two people who spent a year trying to make it go away before anyone was entitled to say
so.
\end{funfact}

\section{The Drake Equation}\label{sec:drake}

In 1961, ahead of a small conference at Green Bank, West Virginia, Frank Drake wrote an
equation on a blackboard. He was not attempting to calculate an answer. He was attempting to
organise an agenda --- to break the question ``how many civilisations are there?'' into
components that could be discussed separately. It has since been printed on more t-shirts
than any other equation except Einstein's, and misunderstood at a comparable rate.

\begin{center}
$N = R_* \cdot f_p \cdot n_e \cdot f_\ell \cdot f_i \cdot f_c \cdot L$
\end{center}

Read it left to right, and notice the change in the quality of the knowledge as you go.

$R_*$, the rate of star formation in our galaxy, is measured. Roughly one and a half to three
solar masses per year. This is astronomy and it is solid.

$f_p$, the fraction of stars with planets, was a wild guess in 1961 and is now measured.
Before 1992 we knew of no planets outside our solar system. As I write, the confirmed count
exceeds 5,\!800, and the statistical conclusion from the Kepler and TESS surveys is that
planets are the rule rather than the exception. Call it close to one. Drake's colleagues
guessed conservatively and were wrong in the interesting direction.

$n_e$, the average number of habitable planets per system, is being narrowed but is still
soft --- estimates for Earth-size planets in the habitable zones of Sun-like stars range from
roughly 2\% to 20\% of such stars, and the definition of ``habitable'' is doing an enormous
amount of concealed work, as Section~\ref{sec:water} will argue.

$f_\ell$, the fraction of habitable planets where life actually appears, is where the
equation stops being science. We have one data point, and we are it. From a sample of one you
can derive nothing --- the fraction could be one, or it could be one in $10^{40}$, and no
observation currently distinguishes those. The one relevant fact is that life on Earth
appears in the record remarkably early, within a few hundred million years of the surface
becoming habitable, which is at least suggestive that abiogenesis is not desperately
improbable. It is not proof of anything; a lottery winner also notices that they won quickly.

$f_i$, the fraction of life that becomes intelligent, is worse. On Earth it took roughly
four billion years and required a specific and contingent sequence: oxygenation, eukaryotes,
multicellularity, several mass extinctions that cleared incumbents, and a final primate
lineage that got lucky. Complex life may be the bottleneck rather than life itself.

$f_c$, the fraction that develops detectable communication, and $L$, the average lifetime of
such a civilisation, are pure speculation. $L$ is the term everybody skips past and it is
arguably the most important in the equation, because it is the only one we might influence.
Drake's own 1961 estimate produced $N$ of around 10,\!000. Modern authors have published
values from $10^{-10}$ to millions.

\begin{funfact}
The Drake Equation's real function is not arithmetic, it is diagnostic --- it shows you
precisely where the ignorance lives. In 1961 six of the seven terms were unknown. Today two
are measured, one is being measured, and four remain unknown. That is genuine progress on a
question that was thought unanswerable, and it happened entirely on the astronomy side. Every
term we have nailed down was nailed down by building better telescopes, not by arguing.
\end{funfact}

\hi{The honest use of the equation is as a filter on other people's confidence.} Anybody who
quotes you a number for $N$ is telling you about their assumptions concerning $f_\ell$, $f_i$
and $L$, and nothing else. This is why Section~\ref{sec:allevidencebutnoproof} warned about mathematics being used as
rhetoric: multiplying four measurements by three guesses does not yield a measurement. It
yields a guess with better manners.

\section{Fermi's Paradox}\label{sec:fermi}

Over lunch at Los Alamos in 1950, in the middle of a conversation about a
\emph{New Yorker} cartoon, Enrico Fermi asked: ``Where is everybody?'' The question has
become the central problem of the field, and it is a genuine paradox rather than a rhetorical
flourish, because the two halves of it are both well-supported.

\figwrap[r]{0.38}{The Milky Way in plan with the Sun's position marked. Full settlement takes millions of years against an age of billions.}{https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Milky_Way_Arms_ssc2008-10.svg}{fig:galaxyplan}
The first half: the galaxy is old. It is roughly 13 billion years old (see Figure~\ref{fig:galaxyplan}), and there are stars
with planets that predate our Sun by billions of years. Even at speeds we could achieve with
physics we already understand --- a few per cent of light speed, using nuclear pulse or beamed
propulsion --- a civilisation that expanded outward and settled systems which then launched
their own probes would colonise the entire galaxy in something between five and fifty million
years. That is a geological eyeblink against 13 billion. Self-replicating probes would do it
faster and cheaper. So if anybody at all ever started, they should be here already, and we
should be surrounded by evidence.

The second half: we observe nothing. No probes, no megastructures, no radio traffic, no
industrial signatures in exoplanet spectra, no artefacts in the solar system. The silence is
not merely an absence of contact; it is an absence of the physical debris that any expansive
technological civilisation should leave behind.

That is the paradox. The resolutions fall into a few families, and I will give you the ones
that are actually defensible.

\textbf{Nobody is out there.} A term in the Drake equation is catastrophically small. The
``Rare Earth'' argument holds that the specific conditions here --- plate tectonics, a large
stabilising moon, a Jupiter clearing debris, a long-lived magnetic field --- are rare in
combination. Alternatively abiogenesis itself is a fluke.

\textbf{They die first.} The Great Filter, in Robin Hanson's formulation. Somewhere between
dead chemistry and galactic expansion there is a step almost nothing survives. The
uncomfortable question is whether the filter is behind us or ahead of us. If we ever find
independent microbial life nearby, that is genuinely bad news: it moves the filter forward,
towards us.

\textbf{They are quiet.} Broadcasting may be understood, everywhere, as a bad idea ---
the ``dark forest'' argument, which I will treat properly in Section~\ref{sec:guerrilla}.
Or technological maturity may mean efficiency, and efficient civilisations are dim.

\textbf{We cannot see them.} Our search has been absurdly shallow. We have examined a
vanishing fraction of the sky, in a narrow band of frequencies, for a few decades, for one
specific kind of signal. The often-quoted comparison is that all SETI observation to date is
equivalent to sampling a hot tub's worth of the Earth's oceans. Absence of evidence here is
very weak evidence of absence.

\textbf{They are here and we have not established it.} This is the hypothesis that gives this
book its subject matter. I list it honestly and rank it low, for the reason Section~\ref{sec:burdenproof}
requires: it is the option carrying the heaviest burden, and seventy years of trying has not
produced a single artefact that survives independent examination.

\begin{funfact}
The zoo hypothesis and the simulation hypothesis are both technically resolutions of Fermi's
Paradox, and both are unfalsifiable, which puts them in the same category as John Titor's
branching timelines. They can be true. They cannot be investigated. A hypothesis that
forbids no observation is not competing with the others; it has left the competition.
\end{funfact}

\section{The Misconception of Water}\label{sec:water}

``Follow the water'' has been NASA's search strategy for thirty years, and it is a defensible
engineering decision dressed up as a law of nature. The distinction matters.

The genuine case for water is good. It is an excellent solvent, so chemistry happens in it. It
is liquid across a wide temperature range. It is unusual in being less dense as a solid, so
bodies of it freeze from the top and stay liquid underneath. It moderates temperature. And
crucially, hydrogen and oxygen are the first and third most abundant chemically active
elements in the universe, so water is everywhere --- in comets, in nebulae, in interstellar
clouds. If you are designing a search on a budget, water is where you look.

\hi{The misconception is the inference that ran the other way: that because our life needs water,
life needs water.} That is a sample of one, again, and it has quietly narrowed the search in
ways worth naming.

\figwrap[l]{0.42}{Cassini radar mosaic of Titan's northern lakes --- liquid methane and ethane, mapped in detail.}{https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:PIA20023-Titan-NorthPole-Lakes.jpg}{fig:titanlakes}
Alternative solvents are chemically serious, not science fiction. Liquid ammonia is polar,
dissolves many of the same compounds, and stays liquid at temperatures where water is ice.
Liquid methane and ethane exist in enormous quantities on Titan, where the lakes are mapped (see Figure~\ref{fig:titanlakes});
a 2005 paper by Benner and colleagues argued that hydrocarbon solvents actually have
advantages for prebiotic chemistry, since water aggressively hydrolyses many of the molecules
life needs to build. Supercritical carbon dioxide is a good solvent and would be stable on
high-pressure worlds. Formamide, hydrogen fluoride, sulphuric acid --- all have been argued.

And the ``habitable zone'' concept, defined as the orbital band where liquid surface water is
possible, is narrower still than the water criterion. It excludes the most promising
locations in our own solar system. Europa has a subsurface ocean beneath its ice, containing
perhaps twice the water of all Earth's oceans, kept liquid by tidal heating and completely
independent of solar distance. So does Enceladus, which is actively venting that ocean into
space through its south polar plumes, where Cassini flew through and detected water, salts,
silica, methane, molecular hydrogen and complex organics. Ganymede and Callisto probably
have oceans too. None of these bodies is in the habitable zone. All of them are better
candidates than Mars.

\begin{funfact}
Earth's own deep biosphere makes the point. Hydrothermal vent ecosystems run on
chemosynthesis, oxidising hydrogen sulphide, with no sunlight at all --- an entire food web
that does not care whether the star exists. Bacteria have been recovered from kilometres deep
in continental rock, metabolising on timescales of centuries. \emph{Deinococcus radiodurans}
survives radiation doses thousands of times lethal to us. Tardigrades survive vacuum. If our
own single sample of life is this flexible, using our own surface conditions as the template
for the search is an odd choice.
\end{funfact}

Carbon, incidentally, is a much stronger requirement than water, and I want to be fair about
which assumptions actually hold up. Carbon forms four stable bonds, catenates into long
chains and rings, and builds the enormous molecular diversity that any conceivable metabolism
needs. Silicon, its usual proposed substitute, sits below it in the periodic table with the
same valence but forms much weaker bonds to itself, and its oxide is a solid rather than a
gas --- silicon-based respiration would mean exhaling sand. Carbon chauvinism is defensible.
Water chauvinism is a budget decision. Learn to tell the difference, because most popular
writing on astrobiology does not.

\section{Extraterrestrial Biological Entities}\label{sec:extraterrestrialbiologic}

\term{EBE} --- Extraterrestrial Biological Entity --- is a piece of vocabulary that arrived
via the alleged MJ-12 documents and associated 1980s testimony, and which has since been
adopted uncritically by the field. I use it here under protest, because it is a bureaucratic
euphemism that lends institutional weight to claims which have none, and I want to be explicit
that nothing in this section is established.

The folklore has produced a fairly stable taxonomy, and its stability is the interesting part.

\textbf{Greys.} Short, grey-skinned, hairless, enormous black almond eyes, no external ears,
vestigial nose and mouth, thin limbs, large cranium. The dominant type since roughly 1965.

\textbf{Nordics.} Tall, human-like, blond, benevolent, spiritually inclined. Dominant in the
1950s contactee period, essentially extinct in the modern record.

\textbf{Reptilians.} Scaled, upright, hostile, associated with the underground-base and
control-conspiracy material of Chapter~\ref{ch:underground}.

\textbf{Mantids and insectoids.} Tall, thin, described as supervisory, appearing mostly in
later abduction accounts.

\figwrap[r]{0.38}{The standard entity types of the reported corpus, drawn to scale: Grey, Nordic, Reptilian, hairy dwarf.}{https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Alien_grey_scale_diagram.svg}{fig:entitytypes}
Here is what should bother you. \hi{Every one of these is a human being with a parameter adjusted.}
The reported entity types are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:entitytypes}. The Greys are a foetus, or a projection of ageing: enlarged head, large eyes, receding
features, small body. The Nordics are an idealised human, and the fact that their heyday was
the 1950s, in a culture with very particular ideas about who was ideal, is not a coincidence
anybody should have to explain. Reptilians are the reptile-brain fear response wearing
clothes. Nothing in this catalogue is genuinely alien --- there is no entity in the corpus
with radial symmetry, no distributed nervous system, no colonial organism, no silicon
chemistry, nothing that a person could not have imagined by adjusting a face in a mirror.
Compare the actual diversity of life on this planet, where an octopus --- a close relative,
sharing our entire evolutionary history --- has a distributed brain, chromatophore
communication, and three hearts. Real biology is stranger than the entire EBE catalogue.

And there is a hard convergent-evolution question underneath the folklore. Some features are
plausibly universal: bilateral symmetry is efficient for directed movement, sensory organs
cluster near the front and near the brain, and eyes have evolved independently dozens of
times on Earth because photons are worth detecting. Manipulative appendages are likely for a
tool user. But the specifics --- humanoid bipedalism, two arms, a face arranged like ours ---
are contingent on our particular lineage of tetrapods that crawled out of water with four
limbs. Get that far in the argument and you notice that the folklore has assumed away the
entire question.

\subsection{The Wrong Question, Asked for Seventy Years}\label{sec:wrongquestion}

The television treatment of this material comes within one step of the point and then walks
past it. The programme holds up an Assyrian relief, or a serpent-headed god from a codex, or a
photocopied page of the Dulce typescripts, and says --- correctly --- that there is something
very strange about a reptile's head sitting on a human body. That observation is sound. It is
exactly the right thing to notice. Then the reasoning proceeds: reptile, therefore reptilian;
reptilian, therefore Draconian; Draconian, therefore an interstellar empire; empire, therefore
a breeding programme. Four steps, each taken because the previous one was said out loud, and
the actual anomaly abandoned at step one.

The question is not whether extraterrestrial reptiles are interbreeding with humanity.
\hi{The question is why the aliens are all Earth species.} A crocodile's head. A mantis's
thorax. A lion's face. A cobra's hood, a fish's scales, a cat's pupil, and a skin and skull
borrowed from ourselves at eight weeks' gestation. Every one of them an animal from this
planet, and most of them from the same latitudes as the person telling the story. Lyra is some
twenty-five parsecs away and has produced a cat. Draco is not even a physical group of stars,
just a line-of-sight accident of objects at wildly different distances, and it has produced a
crocodile. Whatever else is going on, the taxonomy has a distribution problem: it is drawn from
an Earth zoo, and mostly from the popular end of it.

There are two explanations available and, as far as I can see, only two. Either evolution on a
dozen unrelated worlds, across billions of years of wholly separate history, converged not
merely on bipedalism but on the specific facial architecture of particular terrestrial
families --- crocodilians, cats, mantids, elapid snakes --- which is a claim so extravagant
that nobody in the field has ever troubled to defend it, because stating it plainly is enough
to sink it. Or human beings asked to picture a non-human mind do what human beings have always
done, and take the head off a local animal. One hypothesis requires a galaxy stocked from a
natural history programme. The other requires an imagination, which we are independently
certain exists.

So the breeding programme is not the finding; it is the escape hatch. The oddity that the
programme correctly spotted was never evidence of hybridisation, it was evidence of
authorship, and it points at the author rather than the sky. That is why the last line of every
card in the taxonomy that follows names the terrestrial animal the entity was assembled from. It
is not a joke at the witnesses' expense. It is the datum. Section~\ref{sec:animalheads} then
supplies the control experiment, in the one culture that left us thirty centuries of
documentation explaining precisely how and why it put animal heads on human bodies.

\begin{funfact}
The most likely form for a visitor is the one that never appears in the reports: a machine, or
nothing recognisable as a body at all. Our own first and only interstellar emissaries ---
Pioneer, Voyager, and the New Horizons bus --- are unmanned. As Section~\ref{sec:ai}
argued, biology is a poor engineering choice for interstellar travel: it needs atmosphere,
food, radiation shielding, and a return trip, and it dies. The occupant literature has
spent seventy years describing pilots for craft that would not need any.
\end{funfact}

What about actual candidate biology? There is a small amount of real science worth knowing
about. The Viking labelled-release experiment on Mars in 1976 produced a positive result that
has never been fully explained, though the consensus attributes it to perchlorate chemistry in
the soil. The 1996 claim of microfossils in Martian meteorite ALH~84001 has not held up ---
the magnetite chains and carbonate globules are now generally attributed to non-biological
processes --- but it prompted a generation of work on how you would recognise biology if you
found it, and that methodological legacy outlived the claim. The 2020 detection of phosphine
in Venus's atmosphere generated enormous excitement and has been substantially contested on
re-analysis of the data. Enceladus remains the best target in the solar system and nobody
has been back.

That is the state of the evidence for extraterrestrial life: several intriguing signals, all
of them contested, none of them confirmed. It is a considerably weaker position than the
confident taxonomies of the abduction literature would suggest, and considerably more
interesting, because every item on that short list is being actively worked on by people who
publish their errors.

\section{Alien Races: An A\ndash{}Z}\label{sec:racesaz}

The previous section dealt with the four entity types that turn up in the reported corpus ---
the cases with witnesses, dates and files. What follows is something else entirely: the
\emph{taxonomy}. Somewhere between the 1950s contactee circuit and the channelling boom of the
1990s, ufology stopped describing occupants and started compiling a bestiary, complete with
home star systems, political alliances, treaty histories and factional grudges. Depending on
whose list you buy, there are fifty-seven species, or eighty, or one hundred and twenty-one.
Nobody has ever explained where the number comes from.

I have set out forty of the most frequently cited below, alphabetically, with a
portrait of each as the literature describes it. I want to be unambiguous about what these
pictures are: \hi{they are illustrations of claims, not of evidence.} Not one entry on this
list has a specimen, a photograph, a tissue sample, or a witness who can be cross-examined
under oath. Most of them originate in a single book, a single channelling session, or a single
self-published website, and then propagate by citation until they acquire the texture of
reference material.

Read the taxonomy in one sitting, though, and a pattern emerges that is more interesting than
any individual entry. Every single one of these beings is assembled out of terrestrial
biology. Not merely humanoid --- specifically, traceably borrowed: this one is a mantis, that
one a crocodile, this one a lion, that one a fish, that one an ant, and a great many of them
are simply a person with the contrast turned up. The claimed light-years are decorative. The
anatomy never leaves the planet. In the \textsc{Earth stock} line under each entry I have
named the terrestrial creature the description was cut from.

There is exactly one exception in the taxonomy of aliens below that stands out from the rest.
It's likely not the one you're thinking of, because I haven't yet taught you the criteria ---
the lens to filter these images through. You won't find out until you reach the
\emph{About the Author} section of this book, at the very \hi{end}.

under it.

\begin{funfact}
The taxonomy's addresses are worse than its anatomy. Vega, Sirius and the Pleiades are all
young, hot, fast-rotating stars --- between 100 and 450 million years old, when Earth needed
four billion to produce anything that could write. Wolf 424 has no habitable zone. Clarion
cannot exist where it is claimed to be. Whoever built this catalogue picked the stars that
look brightest from a suburban garden, which is a fact about the observer, not the sky.
\end{funfact}

\subsection{One Body, Many Variations}\label{sec:onebody}

Before leaving the taxonomy, count the legs.

Forty races. Forty pairs of legs. Every single entity in the catalogue ---
the mantid, the crocodile lord, the lion warrior, the fish-god, the ant, the feathered
giant, the octopus-headed diver, the being of pure light that supposedly has no body at all and is nevertheless drawn
standing up --- walks upright on two legs, with two arms, one head, and a face on the front
of it. Ourselves included. The bestiary spans a claimed fifty light-years and twelve
unrelated biospheres, and it did not produce one quadruped, one hexapod, one swimmer, one
thing that rolls, floats, branches, or has no fixed shape at all.

That is a remarkable result, and there are only two ways to read it.

The conventional reading --- mine, for what it is worth --- is that the taxonomy is a product
of human imagination, and human imagination is very bad at leaving the human body behind.
We draw what we are. The bipedal monopoly is a fact about illustrators, not about aliens.

But consider the other possibility, because it is the one that actually follows if you take
the reports at face value. \hi{Suppose the bipedal monopoly is real.} Suppose the witnesses
are describing what they saw, the channelers what they were told, and the taxonomy is a fair
sample of what is out there. Then the pattern is not an artefact --- it is the single most
significant datum in the whole of ufology, and it says something very specific about how
life was made.

Because a designer who works this way is recognisable. Engineers do not redesign a chassis
for every model. They settle on one that works and then vary it: same frame, different
cladding. \hi{God's First Principles:} One torso, upright, freeing two limbs for manipulation while two carry the weight;
a head at the top where the sensors get the best view; the whole thing bilaterally
symmetrical so it can be built from one half mirrored. Get that right once and everything
afterwards is a trim option --- scale it up to ten feet or down to four, swap skin for
scales or feathers or chitin, adjust the eyes, add horns, subtract hair, change the colour,
file down the nose. Which is, entry by entry, precisely what this taxonomy is. Not
forty inventions. \hi{One invention, modified forty times.}

And notice what is missing, because the absence is as informative as the pattern. Popular
culture has no trouble imagining bodies unlike ours. It has given us the shapeless
symbiote that pours itself over a host and reassembles at will, the intelligent ocean, the
swarm that thinks as a swarm, the crystalline lattice, the gas cloud with opinions. Every
one of those is available to any witness who has been to the cinema, and not one of them
ever turns up in a sighting report. Nobody comes back from a lonely road at two in the
morning describing an amorphous black liquid that had no fixed outline and spoke without a
mouth. They come back describing something with a head, shoulders, two arms and two legs
--- frightening in its detail, conventional in its architecture. The imagination is
demonstrably capable of the alternatives; the testimony refuses them. If you hold that the
reports are worthless, that refusal is simply the deep grip of the human silhouette on the
human mind. If you hold that the reports are worth anything at all, then the witnesses are
telling us, with a consistency no hoax network could coordinate, that whatever is out there
is built the way we are built.

Follow that to its conclusion and the taxonomy of Section~\ref{sec:racesaz} collapses --- not
into fraud, but into something stranger. There are no alien \emph{races}. There is one
species, distributed, with an enormous range of genetic variation, and we are a variant of
it. The Nordics are not a separate people who happen to look human; they look human because
they are. The Greys are not visitors who happen to resemble a human foetus; they resemble it
because the template is the same and the expression of it differs. What ufology has spent
seventy years cataloguing as interstellar diplomacy would then be something closer to a
family reunion --- distant cousins, badly out of touch, all built off one drawing.

And it explains the interbreeding, which is otherwise the most absurd claim in the entire
literature. Hybridisation between organisms from separate biospheres is not difficult, it is
meaningless: we cannot cross a human with an oak tree and we share four billion years of
ancestry with the oak. The abduction accounts insist on hybrids anyway, obstinately,
thousands of times over. If they are describing anything at all, they are describing
varieties of one stock --- which is the only circumstance in which the claim is even
coherent.

I believe the taxonomy is real. I have spent this chapter explaining why I do. But
I would rather leave you with the harder question than the easy verdict, so here it is. Every
being in this catalogue was drawn from a single body plan, and either that tells you
everything about the poverty of human invention, or it tells you everything about the
method of whoever did the inventing. \hi{One body. Two arms, two legs. Modified at will.}
Those are the only two options, and the weight of this chapter's evidence falls on the second.

Keep this question open, because the tradition does not let it drop. When we reach
Section~\ref{sec:nephilim} we will find the same idea in its oldest surviving form ---
beings from elsewhere taking human wives and producing children of unusual stature --- and
the reason that claim is worth taking seriously as a \emph{text}, whatever one concludes
about it as history, is precisely the argument of this section. Interbreeding across
biospheres is incoherent. Interbreeding within one modified body plan is not. The ancient
version and the modern one make the identical assumption, three thousand years apart,
without either having reason to know of the other.

Which, if you have read this far, is rather the point of the exercise.
